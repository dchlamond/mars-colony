## A short classroom session

Allow roughly 15–20 minutes including discussion. The first mission targets 8–12 minutes of child play; that duration still needs real observation. There is no penalty for reading slowly or discussing a decision. For ages 7–8, offer reading help and use the map instrument. Older children can read independently and choose first-person fieldwork.

Before the session, load the game once and check the device controls. Use **DRB1-20261114-0** for the shared Arcadia mission. Keep the same code, version and starting conditions when comparing results. Use a fresh test profile for a new first attempt; do not erase a child's existing save to obtain a cleaner result. For repeat play, the game labels practice separately.

Three outcomes to look for:

1. The child can explain that a machine shares limited power, supplies and workers with other machines.
2. The child uses an observation to make or revise a preparation/shelter choice.
3. The child understands that delayed communication requires some decisions to be made locally.

### Facilitation script

| Moment | Say or do | Observe |
|---|---|---|
| Before playing | “Two machines need power, but there is not enough for both. What might happen?” | Record the child's reasoning without teaching the answer first. |
| Start | “You are helping six adult explorers. Your first job is to make a prediction and prepare.” | Time to the first independent meaningful action. |
| Preparation | Let the child choose. Ask “What do you expect that to change?” | Whether cost, stored energy, generation or workers enter their explanation. |
| Instrument | Let the child predict, then compare open ground and the shelter marker. The map buttons and Walk on Mars investigate the same teaching model. | Whether they update a mistaken prediction after seeing evidence. |
| Radio quiet period | “Earth cannot reply yet. What can your crew decide here?” | Whether the child mistakes radio loss for darkness or power loss. |
| Result | “What happened to your supplies? What do you think caused that?” | Whether the explanation refers to an actual game result. |
| One changed choice | Let the child choose an alternative. Explain that later choices and outside events repeat, then let the replay run. | Whether they understand that one preparation changed. |
| Transfer | Ask the three questions below. Let the child answer before giving feedback. | Whether they can use the idea in a new situation, rather than repeat a button label. |

If help is needed, use the lightest useful prompt: ask what the current instruction says, point to the relevant meter, then demonstrate only if necessary. Record help instead of treating it as a failure. If reserves run out, use Retry and discuss what could change. Junior crew are not killed; the shortage pauses progression and permits another plan.

## Transfer questions and interpretation

Do not show this answer guide before the child answers.

| New situation | Question | Evidence of understanding |
|---|---|---|
| Another greenhouse | “You want more food. What else should you check before turning on another greenhouse?” | Connects extra food to power, water and/or a worker; checks whether existing systems have enough. |
| More panel dust | “Your battery is full, but dust keeps blocking sunlight. Can the battery make new energy forever?” | Distinguishes storage from generation; expects the reserve to run down and suggests reducing demand, cleaning or restoring generation. |
| A late Earth reply | “A machine stops and Earth will reply later. What could the crew do safely while they wait?” | Uses current readings/reserves and a local plan; does not assume the radio pause itself removes sunlight. |

Optional formative rubric: **0** = no explanation or unrelated guess; **1** = names a relevant resource or action; **2** = explains the resource/action and its consequence. Keep each response distinct. This small rubric is a discussion aid, not a validated achievement test.

A wrong prediction can be a good learning opportunity. Record the first answer and any revised explanation; do not replace the first answer with the coached answer. Fame is a game feedback system, not proof of learning or a school grade.

## How to report a game defect

Record the visible mission code, game version, day, screen/choice, exact sequence that caused the problem, expected behavior and actual behavior. Export the anonymous mission record if possible. Use screenshots of the game only, avoiding identifiable browser/account details. An unsuccessful strategy is not automatically a bug: a clearly explained shortage with a working retry is intended. Falling through terrain, unreachable controls, unexplained stuck progression or a failed save are defects to reproduce.

## Science language to use

- A dosimeter studies ionizing radiation; it is not a thermometer.
- The outdoor/shelter readings are **simulated relative indices**, not live Mars measurements or an astronaut dose/protection assessment.
- MOLA data informs the terrain; local relief and production formulas are simplified.
- The cached Horizons one-way light time informs the advice queue. The game compresses its message clock; a Martian day is not twenty minutes.
- Radio quiet does not turn off sunlight. Dust and radiation exercises have separate schedules.
- The game uses short growth times, resource units, machine costs and preparation bonuses for teaching. They are not NASA engineering specifications.

See [the implemented rules and provenance](../research/BLACKOUT-RULES.md) and the in-game NASA evidence panel. The short mission introduces power, reserves, radiation and delayed communication; the longer Junior campaign develops building, food production, water recovery and population growth.
